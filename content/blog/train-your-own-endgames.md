## You reached a winning endgame and did not win

Every club player knows the feeling. You outplayed your opponent for forty moves, traded into an endgame a piece or two pawns up, and then the scoresheet says draw — or worse. Nothing dramatic happened. The advantage leaked away one imprecise king move at a time, and by the time you noticed, the win was gone.

Those games hurt more than the ones you lost to a tactic, because you had already done the hard part. And they are worth studying for exactly that reason: a dropped endgame is half a point or a full point that was already yours.

## The problem with studying endgames from a book

The classic advice is to work through an endgame manual — Dvoretsky, or *100 Endgames You Must Know*. Those books are excellent, and the theoretical positions in them (Lucena, Philidor, key squares) are worth knowing cold. But book study has a gap: the positions in your games are messier than the textbook ones, and knowledge you never apply decays. You can know the Philidor position perfectly and still lose a rook endgame two pawns up, because the losing moment came fifteen moves before the theory applied.

Guided drills like Lichess Practice have the same limitation from the other side. They teach fundamentals well, on positions drawn from a curriculum. Your weak spots live in the specific endings you reach and misplay.

## Play it out against real resistance

An endgame is a sequence of decisions under resistance, so a single-move puzzle cannot test it. The honest test is to take a position you should have won (or held), sit down against an opponent who never blunders, and keep the result on the board move after move.

That is what an engine is for. Play the position out against full strength, and let your [winning chances](/about) be the judge: hold the deserved result for ten of your own moves, or finish the game, and you have passed. Let the evaluation slip below the line and you have found the exact move where your technique breaks down.

## A routine that fixes your own endings

1. Go through your recent games and collect the endgames where the engine says you were winning or holding, and the result says otherwise.
2. Replay each one from just before the first slip, against the engine at full strength.
3. When you drop the win or the draw, look at the refutation, then retry from the mistake — your progress up to that point still counts.
4. Feed the positions you misplay into a [spaced-repetition queue](/blog/what-is-the-woodpecker-method) so they come back until they are automatic.
